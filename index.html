Chapter 1

Quantum Mechanics

Quantum theory predicts probabilities for measurement outcomes with extraordinary accuracy. It does not describe electrons as tiny planets following ordinary classical orbits.

ψ
Superposition

A quantum state can be a linear combination of possible basis states. Interference between amplitudes is observable.

Born rule

The probability density for a position measurement is proportional to |ψ|². Amplitudes can interfere; probabilities are obtained after taking the squared magnitude.

Uncertainty

For position and momentum, ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2. This is a property of quantum states, not merely bad measuring equipment.

Entanglement

Composite quantum states can contain correlations that cannot be reproduced by local hidden-variable models. Entanglement does not permit faster-than-light messaging.

Quantization

Some observables have discrete allowed values. Atomic bound-state energies are a familiar example.

Measurement

Quantum theory assigns outcome probabilities through measurement operators. The interpretation of what measurement means physically remains an active foundational question.

Evidence

Experiments that forced physics to change

Double-slit interference

Single quantum particles can accumulate an interference pattern statistically when path information is not available.

Photoelectric effect

Electron emission depends on photon frequency, supporting energy quanta E = hf rather than a purely classical wave-energy picture.

Stern–Gerlach

A beam of atoms splits into discrete components in an inhomogeneous magnetic field, revealing quantized angular momentum projections.

Bell tests

Experiments violate Bell inequalities in agreement with quantum mechanics, ruling out broad classes of local hidden-variable theories.

Chapter 2

Relativity

Special relativity connects space and time when gravity can be neglected. General relativity describes gravity as spacetime geometry.

c
Invariant speedc = 299,792,458 m/s

The SI definition fixes this value exactly in vacuum.

Mass–energyE₀ = mc²

1 gram of rest mass corresponds to about 8.99×10¹³ joules.

Lorentz factorγ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)

γ controls time dilation and length contraction between inertial frames.

General relativityGμν + Λgμν = 8πG Tμν / c⁴

In compact form, matter-energy tells spacetime how to curve.

Relativity of simultaneity

Observers moving relative to one another can disagree about whether spatially separated events happened at the same time.

No massive object reaches c

For an object with nonzero rest mass, the energy required to reach c diverges as γ increases without bound.

Gravity changes clock rates

Clocks deeper in a gravitational potential run more slowly relative to clocks farther away. GPS systems must account for relativistic clock effects.

Gravitational waves

Accelerating asymmetric mass distributions can launch propagating distortions of spacetime that travel at c.

Quantum field theory

Particles are not little billiard balls

Fields fill space

Electron, quark, photon, and other fields exist throughout spacetime. A particle is a quantized excitation associated with a field.

Virtual particles

Internal lines in perturbative calculations are mathematical components of an approximation scheme; they are not directly detected free particles.

Vacuum is not “nothing”

The quantum vacuum is the lowest-energy state of the fields and still has measurable quantum structure.

Gravity is the outlier

General relativity is extraordinarily successful classically, but a complete experimentally confirmed quantum theory of gravity is still absent.
